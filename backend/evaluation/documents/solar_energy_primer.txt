Solar Energy Primer
Revision SEP-11, prepared for the renewables reading group.

1. How a cell produces current

A photovoltaic cell is a semiconductor sandwich. Sunlight arriving at the
junction knocks electrons loose from the crystal lattice, and the built-in
electric field at the boundary between the p-type and n-type layers pushes
those freed carriers in one direction rather than letting them recombine. The
result is a flow of charge that an external circuit can do work with. Nothing
in the cell moves; there is no combustion and no rotating machinery, which is
why the failure modes of a panel are so different from those of a turbine.

2. Efficiency in the field

A laboratory record and a rooftop yield are not the same number. Commercial
monocrystalline silicon modules sold in 2024 convert between 20 and 22 percent
of incident light. The best multi-junction cells built for satellites reach 47
percent, but they cost roughly forty times as much per watt and are never
deployed terrestrially. Panel output falls as the panel heats: the temperature
coefficient of a typical silicon module is -0.35 percent per degree Celsius
above 25 degrees, so a roof at 60 degrees in midsummer gives up around 12
percent of its rated output on the hottest and sunniest afternoons.

3. Degradation

Modules lose capacity slowly. The industry rule of thumb is 0.5 percent per
year, which is where the standard twenty-five-year warranty figure of 80
percent of nameplate output comes from. Degradation is not linear: there is a
sharp initial drop called light-induced degradation, typically 1 to 3 percent
within the first thousand hours of exposure, after which the curve flattens
into the slow annual decline.

4. Storage pairing

Storage changes the economics more than it changes the physics. A lithium
iron phosphate battery paired with a residential array is sized for the
evening peak rather than for the whole night, because the last few kilowatt
hours of autonomy are the most expensive ones to buy. Round-trip efficiency
for a well-managed LFP system is 90 to 95 percent, and the cells tolerate
roughly 6000 full cycles before falling to 80 percent of their original
capacity. Depth of discharge matters more than cycle count alone: shallow
cycling extends calendar life considerably.

5. What limits a single-junction cell

The Shockley-Queisser limit sets the ceiling. A single-junction silicon cell
cannot exceed 33.7 percent conversion under unconcentrated sunlight, because
photons below the band gap pass straight through unabsorbed and photons well
above it waste their surplus energy as heat. Multi-junction stacks beat the
limit only by stacking materials with different band gaps so that each layer
harvests a different slice of the spectrum.
