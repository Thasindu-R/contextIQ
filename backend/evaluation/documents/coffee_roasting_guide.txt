A Practical Guide to Roasting Coffee
Notes from the roastery bench, third edition.

The phases of a roast

A roast moves through three phases. Drying runs from charge until the beans turn
yellow and lose most of their free moisture. The Maillard phase follows, where
sugars and amino acids react to build the aromatic compounds that give roasted
coffee its character. Development is the final phase, beginning at first crack
and ending at drop. Development time as a share of total roast time is the
number most roasters watch; between 20 and 25 percent suits most washed coffees.

First crack and second crack

First crack is an audible popping caused by water vapour and carbon dioxide
escaping as the bean's structure gives way, and it happens near 196 degrees
Celsius. Second crack, near 224 degrees, is a quieter, sharper sound as the cell
walls themselves fracture and oils migrate to the surface. Roasting into second
crack pushes flavour toward smoke and bitterness and away from the character of
the origin, which is why lighter roasts are preferred for single-origin lots
with distinctive acidity.

Rate of rise

Rate of rise is how fast bean temperature climbs, usually read in degrees per
minute. A healthy curve declines steadily throughout the roast. If the rate of
rise flattens completely, the roast has crashed and the resulting cup tastes
flat and papery; if it rises again after first crack, the flip produces harsh
and ashy notes. Neither fault can be corrected after the fact.

Degassing and rest

Freshly roasted beans release carbon dioxide for days. Brewing too soon gives an
uneven extraction because the escaping gas pushes water away from the grounds.
Filter coffee is usually best between 4 and 14 days after roasting; espresso
benefits from a longer rest, commonly 10 to 21 days, because the pressure of an
espresso shot exaggerates the effect of trapped gas.

Storage

Store beans whole, in an opaque airtight container with a one-way valve, at
stable room temperature. Oxygen is the main enemy, followed by heat, light, and
humidity in that order. Freezing works and works well, but only in sealed
single-dose portions taken out once and never refrozen, because condensation on
thawed beans ruins them. Grinding immediately before brewing matters more than
any storage choice: ground coffee stales within hours.

The Ristretto Kilimanjaro lot

The 2024 Ristretto Kilimanjaro lot is a washed Tanzanian peaberry roasted to an
end temperature of 208 degrees with a development ratio of 22 percent. It cups
at 86.5 points, with tasting notes of blackcurrant, bergamot, and brown sugar.
It is the roastery's recommendation for filter brewing and is not offered as an
espresso.
